Case study · Medical aesthetics

An appointment that never happened became a "botched Botox" review. Here is how it came down.

A medical spa offering injectables and laser treatments picked up a detailed 1-star review describing a Botox and filler appointment gone wrong, complete with a named complication. Its booking system and payment records showed no client matching that visit. Google's own flagging tool rejected the appeal. Here is what happened after that, and what it means for any aesthetics or elective medical business fighting a review it cannot verify.

A medical spa selling injectables and laser treatments lives and dies by a decision most clients make once and research hard before they book: who gets to put a needle near their face. That research almost always starts on Google, before a name, a price, or a single photo. The case in this piece began with a 1-star review from an account that had never posted a review before, describing a Botox and filler appointment with a specific complication. The medspa checked its booking system. No client matched the name, the date, or the treatment described.

The dispute: a review with no matching appointment

The medspa in this example is a single-location practice offering Botox, dermal filler, and laser skin treatments, run by a nurse practitioner who serves as medical director and owner. One afternoon, a 1-star review appeared on its Google Business Profile, posted by an account with no other reviews attached to it. The review described an appointment for Botox in the forehead and filler in the lips, and it named a specific date, a charge of $650 for the visit, and a complication: a drooping brow and uneven lips that, according to the review, took weeks to settle.

Front-desk staff pulled the appointment calendar for that date. No client under that name had booked, called, or shown up. The injector named in the review had taken the day off that week for a family event, according to the staff schedule, and had not treated a single client at the practice that week. Payment records for the date in question showed no transaction matching the $650 charge cited in the review. Three separate systems, the booking calendar, the staff schedule, and the point-of-sale log, agreed on the same fact: the appointment described in the review did not happen.

Why does a single fake review hit a medspa harder than most businesses?

Because a prospective client comparing injectable providers treats a described bad outcome as a preview of their own risk, not as one stranger's opinion. Choosing who injects a needle near your eyes or lips is a different decision than picking a restaurant for dinner. Clients read the available reviews before they book a consultation, and a specific, clinical-sounding complication, drooping, asymmetry, an allergic reaction, reads as evidence rather than a complaint about slow service. Consultation requests at this medspa fell 31% over the three weeks the review stayed live, compared with the same three-week stretch a month earlier.

Why didn't Google's flagging tool catch a review with zero reviewer history?

Because Google's automated flagging system checks review text against policy categories, not against whether the account posting it has a pattern worth a second look. The medspa flagged the review within two days of it posting, citing that no matching appointment existed in its records. About a week later, the appeal came back rejected with a generic notice: "no policy violation found." An account that had posted one review, ever, aimed at a single business, with a level of clinical detail a first-time reviewer would be unlikely to invent from nothing, did not trip any automated check. Google's system reads review text for banned language. It does not cross-reference an account's history against the business being reviewed, and it does not ask the business whether the described visit took place.

What happened next

After the Google flag came back rejected, the practice pulled full documentation: the booking calendar export, the staff schedule showing the named injector was off that week, and the point-of-sale log for the date in question. The owner sent this along with the review link to Lizard Reviews. We reviewed the case within a day and accepted it. A review describing a specific, clinical-sounding event that documented records show never happened supports a fabricated-content policy violation once it is framed as a false claim rather than a dispute over service quality.

Why elective aesthetic providers carry more exposure here

Two things work against a medical spa or injectable provider that a coffee shop or a plumber almost never deals with. First, the subject matter invites belief. A complication after Botox or filler is a real, documented risk of any injectable procedure, uncommon but real, so a description of drooping or asymmetry lands as believable even when invented from nothing. A reader cannot tell an account written by a disappointed client apart from one written by a competitor, a person with a grudge, or someone who confused two businesses with similar names, because the clinical language reads the same either way.

Second, the practice cannot fight with its strongest evidence in public. A medical spa holds the exact records that would settle the dispute in minutes, consent forms, treatment charts, before-and-after photos, but those records belong to real clients and cannot be published to prove a point about someone who was never a client at all. The medspa in this example could show, with full confidence, that its own systems held no record of the visit described. It could not post its booking calendar as a public reply without exposing real client data in the process. That gap, holding proof you are not allowed to show, is what makes a fabricated review this hard to fight without a channel built to review documentation and argue the policy violation instead of relitigating it in a reply box under the listing.

That is the pattern worth remembering for any elective, appearance-related medical business: a claim against you does not need truth to do damage, only plausibility. A fabricated bad-outcome story about a Botox or filler visit reads as believable to a stranger scrolling reviews before a consultation, whether or not the visit ever took place. The business holds the records that would end the argument. It cannot publish them. That mismatch, not a shortage of good reviews or a slow response time, is the real vulnerability.
