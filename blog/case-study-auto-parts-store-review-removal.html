A fake review accused an auto parts store of selling counterfeit brakes. The sale never happened.

A composite case study of an independent auto parts store, a one-star review claiming counterfeit brake pads, and what the sales log showed when the owner went looking for the transaction.

CASE SUMMARY

A one-star review accused an auto parts store of selling counterfeit brake pads. The sales log showed no matching purchase. Lizard Reviews got it removed in about a week.

  • The review: one star, describing a $164 brake pad purchase the reviewer called counterfeit, posted by an account with no other Google activity.
  • The stakes: direction requests and calls from the store's Google listing fell by about 30% over three weeks, and two wholesale mechanic accounts paused their next order until it was resolved.
  • The dead end: Google's flagging tool returned "no policy violation found," even though the store had no sales record matching the date, amount, or part described.

A note on this case study
This is a composite example built from patterns we see across real removal cases we handle for U.S. retailers, not a single identifiable client. The business is described generically (an independent auto parts store carrying both new and aftermarket parts), and the owner appears under a first-name pseudonym, "Ray." Names, locations, and other identifying details have been changed or generalized to protect confidentiality. The numbers, the sequence of events, and the outcome reflect what happens in cases like this one.

The review posted on a Thursday morning: one star, five sentences, describing a brake pad set bought for $164 that the reviewer called "obviously counterfeit" and said wore down to metal within two weeks of installation. It closed with a line telling other customers not to trust the store. Ray, who has run the shop for nine years, read it standing at the counter and walked straight back to the point-of-sale terminal to pull the transaction.

The purchase that never showed up in the sales log

Nothing matched. No brake pad set sold for $164 on the date named in the review, and none in the two days on either side of it. No customer under the name on the reviewer's account, in the point-of-sale system or the loyalty program the store runs for regular buyers. No warranty claim or return logged either, and the store logs every return, because manufacturer warranties on brake components require proof of purchase before a claim goes through. A customer who bought defective pads and brought them back would leave a paper trail. This one left none.

About four miles away, in a neighboring town, another parts retailer operates under a name close enough to cause mix-ups. Ray had taken calls before from people who thought they'd reached the other store. A reviewer who bought pads somewhere else and left the complaint on the wrong Google listing is one explanation. A review with no basis in any transaction at all is another, no less plausible than the first. Either way, the accusation sat on a listing that had never sold the part described, to the customer named, at the price quoted.

Why "counterfeit" hits harder than a bad-service complaint

A one-star review about slow service or a rude clerk is a nuisance that fades with the next good one. A review claiming counterfeit brake pads is a safety accusation aimed at the product itself, not the experience of buying it. Brake components that aren't genuine can fail, and customers know that. Auto parts is a category where a buyer, in most cases, can't verify the product by looking at it, so they lean on the seller's reputation to make that call for them. A single review claiming counterfeit parts goes after the one thing a parts store sells: the assurance that what's on the shelf is real.

The store's Google listing carried a 4.6 average across 58 reviews before this one posted, high enough that one star moved the number by less than a tenth of a point. That didn't matter much, because Google's default sort surfaces recent reviews near the top of the listing, so for two weeks this one sat in the first screen anyone saw when they tapped in.

What it cost at the counter

In the three weeks after the review went up, direction requests from the store's Google Business Profile fell by about 30% against the same three weeks the month before, and calls from the listing dropped by a comparable margin. Two of the store's commercial accounts, independent mechanics who buy pads, rotors, and filters on standing orders, called to ask about the review before placing their next order. One held the order for nine days. Retail walk-ins and wholesale mechanic accounts split the store's revenue in close to equal shares, so a stall on either side shows up in the weekly numbers fast.

What Ray tried first

Ray used Google's own flagging tool the week the review appeared, selecting "not a customer" as the reason and adding a note that the store had no record of the purchase. Nine days later the reply came back: no policy violation found. The review used no profanity and made no threats, so it never tripped the automated filters built to catch obvious violations. A false accusation of selling counterfeit goods isn't the kind of thing those filters are built to catch on their own. Confirming that no matching sale exists takes someone comparing the claim against store records, and the first-pass automated system doesn't do that.

"I had the point-of-sale system open right in front of me, showing nothing sold that day for that amount, and Google's answer was still no violation found. At that point I wasn't after an apology. I wanted the review gone before it cost us another wholesale order." "Ray," composite owner quote representing a pattern from real cases

The broader lesson for retailers selling anything customers can't verify themselves

Auto parts stores share a vulnerability with a specific set of other retailers: electronics resellers, supplement shops, anyone selling a product where the buyer can't confirm authenticity by looking at it. A "counterfeit" or "ripped off" accusation is one of the most damaging claims a reviewer can make against that kind of business, and one of the hardest to fight in public, because proving it false means showing sales records that most owners are reluctant to post in a reply to a stranger online. High damage paired with a low ability to self-defend in public makes these businesses frequent targets, whether the review traces back to confusion with another store, a competitor, or someone with no relationship to the business at all.

The instinct to prove who wrote the review and why is understandable, and there are legitimate ways to try to identify an anonymous reviewer. That path moves slower than removal in most cases, and it doesn't take the review off the listing while it plays out. Getting the review down first, then sorting out the rest, is what stops the bleeding at the counter.

Frequently asked questions

Why do auto parts stores get targeted by fake counterfeit-parts reviews?

Auto parts stores sell a product a buyer can't verify by looking at it, so a claim of counterfeit goods is hard to disprove in public and does more damage than an ordinary complaint. That combination makes parts retailers, along with electronics and supplement sellers, a common target for this exact kind of false review.

Can a business prove a Google review is fake without exposing customer data?

To a removal service, yes. A sales log or point-of-sale export showing no transaction matching the date, amount, or item named in the review is strong evidence, and it can be shared in private, without posting it in public or naming other customers.

Why didn't Google's flagging tool catch a review with no matching purchase record?

Google's automated review of flagged content looks for specific violations such as profanity, spam links, or threats. A calm, false accusation doesn't trip those filters on its own. Confirming that no matching sale exists takes a human comparing the claim against store records, and the first-pass automated system isn't built to do that.

How much can one fake review affect a local retail store's foot traffic?

It depends on the store's existing review base and how much of its business comes through Google search and Maps. In this case, direction requests and calls from the store's listing fell by about 30% over three weeks, and two wholesale mechanic accounts paused orders until the review was resolved.
