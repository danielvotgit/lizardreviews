Type the question into Google and the answers range from free to five figures, and most of them are wrong for your situation. What removal costs depends on what's wrong with the review, who's doing the removing, and whether the provider gets paid whether or not anything happens. Here's what businesses actually pay in 2026, broken down by method, including the gaps most sales pages leave out.

This isn't a ranking of which service is "best." It's a straight look at the price tags across the industry: what a flat fee buys, what pay-on-success changes about the risk you're carrying, and when the bill jumps into the thousands because a lawyer got involved.

Three pricing models in this industry

Almost every provider you'll find falls into one of three structures.

Flat fee per review. You pay a set amount whether Google removes the review or not. Prices span $150 to $2,000 depending on the vendor and how aggressively they market themselves, and a fair number of these outfits fold before delivering anything at all.

Performance-based, pay only on success. You pay nothing until the review is confirmed gone. This is the model most reputable operators use, because it puts the risk where it belongs: the provider only gets paid if the work actually happens. Pricing in this band typically runs $299 to $1,500 per review, scaled by how contested the case is.

Monthly retainer or subscription. Built for businesses with an ongoing review problem rather than one bad review. You pay monthly for monitoring plus removal attempts on whatever surfaces, which fits a multi-location brand better than a single storefront dealing with one review.

What actually moves the price within those bands

Three factors explain most of the spread. Case difficulty: an obvious policy violation (spam, impersonation, hate speech) is cheaper to resolve than a nuanced dispute that reads as a genuine bad experience. Volume: agencies and multi-location businesses negotiate per-review rates well below single-case retail pricing. And scope: a rating-only review, a star with no accompanying text, is close to unremovable through any channel, because there's no content to test against a policy, so a fair provider prices it differently or declines it outright.

The $200-500 flagging trap

Some of the cheapest offers at the low end of that flat-fee range do exactly one thing: click the same "Report review" button sitting on your screen right now, for free, then wait. That's DIY flagging with a markup attached. Google's own flagging tool costs nothing and succeeds on roughly 20-40% of clear policy violations, but it's slow (two days to six weeks) and it fails quietly on the reviews that actually hurt: the ones that read as a bad experience rather than an obvious rule break. Paying someone $250 to click a free button doesn't change those odds. Before paying anyone, ask what they do beyond flagging. A vague answer is an answer.

When the price jumps into the thousands: the legal route

If a review survives flagging, survives a support escalation, and survives a professional removal attempt, the remaining lever is legal: a defamation demand letter, a subpoena to unmask an anonymous reviewer, or an actual lawsuit. Attorney fees on this track run $1,000 to $5,000 or more, and the clock moves in months, not days. That price makes sense for a narrow set of high-stakes cases (a false fraud accusation, a licensing complaint, a coordinated attack) but it's a poor fit for the average one-star review costing a business a few thousand dollars a month in lost traffic. How to tell if a price is fair

Price alone tells you almost nothing, because a $300 quote from a service that never delivers is worse value than a $450 quote from one that does. Three questions cut through most of the noise.

Do you pay only if it works? Anyone billing you upfront for a review that might not be removable is asking you to underwrite their guesswork, not sharing the risk with you.

What's the method beyond flagging? If the pitch stops at "we report it for you," you're paying for something you could do yourself in five minutes, badly disguised as a service.

What happens if the review comes back? A provider confident in its own work will warranty it. Ours does, on every invoice, for as long as the removal lasts.
