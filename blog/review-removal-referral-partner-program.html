The email is short. Your client forwards a Google review from someone who was never a customer, or from a competitor, or from an ex-employee still angry about the exit. You flag it, because you run their web presence and this is their web presence. Days later Google replies with the sentence everyone in this line of work can recite: no policy violation found.

Now you write the follow-up, and you have nothing to put in it. You did the right thing through the official channel, the channel said no, and the person reading your message pays you monthly to make exactly this sort of problem disappear. Nothing in that exchange makes you look competent.

Google's own blog reported that it blocked or removed more than 240 million policy-violating reviews in 2024, along with more than 12 million fake Business Profiles. Enforcement at that volume has to be automated, which is why a single rejection reads as arbitrary. Your client does not see a classifier making a call. They see you losing.

Two routes, and both are written down

Most partner pages in this category end at a form. Apply, qualify, and terms will be discussed. That gate exists because the terms are usually worse than the pitch, and a percentage is easier to defend in a call than in public. So here is ours, including the part we cannot answer yet.

Route one, referral. You send the case, we do the work, your client pays us, we pay you. There is no published percentage because we have not fixed one, and printing "10%" here to look decisive would just be a number invented for a blog post. What happens instead: we agree the rate, the cap and the duration in writing before you introduce anyone, and it stays in that document. If that sounds like less certainty than you wanted, it is, and it is still more than a form gives you.

Route two, wholesale. You buy at the partner rate and sell at yours. Direct retail is $450 per review, billed after Google confirms the removal. Partner wholesale is tiered by volume and typically starts in a €300-400 band. Both figures already sit on this site, so you can run the arithmetic at today's rate before you talk to us. Nothing obliges you to price at $450 either. That is our public number, which makes it the ceiling your client can find without you, and a reasonable place to anchor.

What the rest of the market pays

Deciding what to ask for on route one is easier with the practitioner benchmarks in front of you. Agency consultants who benchmark this put the most common referral rate at 10% of revenue, with 5% next. Twelve month caps are typical, and a step-down shows up often: 10% in year one, 5% in year two, then nothing. The same consultants note that agencies paying in perpetuity tend to regret it, usually around the point where they are still paying for an introduction nobody remembers making.

Marketing agency fees commonly run 5% to 15% of first project or contract value. Freelancers sit wider, 5% to 30%. None of this is survey-grade data. It is what people who do this for a living say when asked, and it is enough to keep a conversation from opening somewhere absurd.

Your name travels with the referral

The reason most of these partnerships never produce a single case has nothing to do with the fee. A referral is a loan of your judgment. If the vendor is slow, or vague about what it is doing, or turns up in your client's inbox three weeks later pitching SEO, that lands on you. You picked them.

Which makes the terms about behaviour more load-bearing than the terms about money:

  • Accept or decline within 24 hours. You are never stuck telling a client you are waiting to hear back from someone.
  • Median 4 days to removal, range 3 to 7. Measurable against your own cases.
  • We do not contact your client and we do not compete with you for them. Published policy, not a concession you have to negotiate.
  • Mutual NDA on every partnership, both directions.
  • 0 of 1,427 removed reviews have come back, and a lifetime re-removal warranty is written into every invoice.

Taking a cut from someone who trusts you

The second objection is quieter and rarely said out loud: charging a commission on a client who hired you to be on their side feels like a conflict. It is one, unless you handle it the way your profession already handles it. Disclose the fee before the introduction. Put it in the engagement letter or the scope, not in a message afterwards. Never take payment before your client has actually paid. Bolting a fee on after the fact is what turns an ordinary arrangement into something you would rather your client never discovered.

Plenty of people read that and decline the fee entirely, because being the person who fixed it is the whole return. Defensible, and it changes nothing about how the case gets handled.

The three things your client has been warned about

Buyers in this category get taught to watch for the same three signals, and they are the right three.

Anyone guaranteeing removal of any review. We do not. Rating-only reviews, a star with no text, sit outside what we do, and we say so before you ask. What we publish is the record instead: 1,427 reviews removed between January and May 2026, with a 100% result on reviews that contain text. We tell you inside 24 hours whether we are taking a case. The guarantee is financial rather than predictive, so if we take it and it stays up, no invoice is issued.

Large upfront fees before anyone has looked at the case. There are none, on either route. An invoice exists only after Google confirms the removal.

Anyone claiming insiders at Google. Walk away, and that includes walking away from us if you ever hear it here. We have no contacts inside Google and we do not imply otherwise. What we say about the method is that it is a proprietary, direct removal process, and we publish nothing beyond that. Weigh an undisclosed method as a risk if you like. It sits against an invoice that only arrives on success, which puts most of that risk on our side of the table.

Attorneys: the fee is the wrong instrument

Defamation lawyers get asked about platform removal constantly and mostly do not do it, because a demand letter and a Google policy queue are unrelated machinery. The referral is obvious. The commission is not.

ABA Model Rule 7.2(b) provides that a lawyer shall not "give anything of value to a person for recommending the lawyer's services". Exceptions are narrow, and the one people reach for is the non-exclusive reciprocal referral agreement, which has to stay non-exclusive and be disclosed to the client. The Model Rules are adopted state by state with variations, so the rule that binds you is your state's version, not this paragraph.

Direction of payment matters too. A fee flowing to you from a vendor you steered a client toward raises its own questions about independent professional judgment, separate from 7.2(b) entirely.

So we do not offer attorneys a commission. Two things we do offer: a reciprocal, non-exclusive arrangement where cases move in both directions and no money changes hands, or a straight hand-off where you pass the client our direct line and stay out of the economics completely. Both work. Neither needs a memo to your ethics counsel.
